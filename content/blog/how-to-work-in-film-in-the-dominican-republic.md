To work in film in the Dominican Republic you need four things: be clear about the department you want to break into, register with **SIRECINE** (the official registry of the Dominican Film Commission, DGCINE), have a crew profile or CV that a head of department can review in a minute, and build your first credits on short films, commercials or music videos. The rest comes from reputation: on set, people call back whoever showed up early, solved problems and didn't cause any.

This guide walks through each step, along with what you should know about how the industry works in the country today.

## Why there is film work in the DR

The Dominican Republic has become one of the most active filming destinations in the Caribbean, and that translates into local jobs. According to figures from the Dominican Film Commission (DGCINE) reported by Acento, the industry generated **3,807 jobs in 2025**, and almost 90% went to local talent. DGCINE's registry lists 92 audiovisual productions, and in 2025 alone at least 35 international feature films were shot in the country.

Two factors drive that activity:

- **Film Law 108-10.** Its article 39 (amended by Law 82-13) offers a transferable tax credit equal to 25% of what a production spends in the Dominican Republic. Local crew wages are part of that spending, so productions have a reason to hire people here.
- **Infrastructure.** The studios in Juan Dolio, formerly Pinewood Dominican Republic Studios and now **Lantica Studios**, have hosted productions such as *The Lost City* and *Nyad*, as well as international series. But not everything happens on a stage: most productions also shoot on location in Santo Domingo, Santiago, the east coast and the north.

## How a crew is organized

A production is split into departments, each with a head who builds their own team. Knowing this changes how you look for work: you don't write to "the movie", you write to the head of the department you want to join.

| Department | What it does | Typical entry-level roles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Production | Logistics, permits, transport, catering, budget | Production assistant (PA), runner |
| Locations | Finds and manages filming locations | Locations assistant |
| Directing | Shooting schedule, timing, pace on set | Set PA |
| Camera | Camera gear, focus, cards and footage | Camera trainee (assistant roles require experience) |
| Electric and grip | Lighting, power, camera support | Electric or grip helper |
| Art | Sets, props, set dressing | Art assistant, props assistant |
| Wardrobe and makeup | Characters, visual continuity | Wardrobe assistant, makeup assistant |
| Sound | Recording dialogue and ambience | Sound assistant |
| Post-production | Editing, color, final sound, effects | Assistant editor |

If you're starting from scratch, **production** and **locations** are the most common ways in. They need a lot of people, you learn how the whole set works, and you meet the heads of the other departments. Departments like camera, electric or sound demand a lot of technical knowledge. A background in photography, electrical work or audio points you toward them, but you still start at the bottom, learning from people who know the craft.

## Step 1: Define where you want to start

"I can do anything" sounds flexible, but to the person hiring it says you don't know where you fit. Define the department that interests you and introduce yourself honestly: as someone who wants to gain experience in that area, without overstating what you've done so far.

Being clear about your interest doesn't mean closing yourself off to other areas. Many people start as PAs and end up in directing, art or locations, and doing good work in another department also opens doors (the tips below explain why).

## Step 2: Register with SIRECINE

**SIRECINE** is the official registry run by DGCINE. It is the industry's official database, covering people, companies, works and service providers. Registration is a requirement to take part in the industry and to access the incentives of Law 108-10. Productions that want those incentives need to work with registered people.

Requirements and procedures can change, so check them directly on the [Dominican Film Commission (DGCINE)](https://dgcine.gob.do) website before you start. Do it early: it's one of those things nobody asks for until the day you urgently need it.

## Step 3: Build a crew profile that can be read in a minute

A head of department putting a team together reviews many profiles in little time. Yours should quickly answer three questions: **what you do, what you've done and whether you're available.** Include:

- **Department and role**, at the top and clear.
- **Credits**: project title, type (feature, series, commercial, short), your role, year and production company. Student short films count too.
- **Gear and tools you know**: cameras, lights, sound mixers, editing software or whatever applies to your department.
- **Languages**: English matters on foreign productions, even at a working level, and so does Spanish.
- **Location and mobility**: city, whether you have a vehicle and license, whether you can travel or stay away for several days.
- **A contact you actually answer**, ideally WhatsApp.

## Step 4: Get your first credits

Some ways to build experience:

- **Student and independent short films.** Film schools and collectives always need hands. It's real work under real pressure, and today's directors are the ones hiring tomorrow.
- **Commercials and music videos.** Short, frequent, well-organized shoots. A good school for set rhythm and discipline.
- **Training with hands-on practice.** In 2020, INFOTEP (the national vocational training institute) signed agreements with the Caribbean Film Entertainment Association (ECC-RD) and Lantica Studios for technical training and film internships. Ask INFOTEP and film schools about current openings.
- **Offer support to a head of department.** Write to them directly, with your profile and one clear line about what you can do for their team.

## Step 5: Your reputation is your best résumé

In film, most positions are filled through recommendations. When a head of department builds a team, they first call the people they've already worked with. What you do on your first shoot decides whether you get called for the second.

- **Arrive before your call time.** On set, on time is late.
- **Read the call sheet** the night before: schedule, location, scenes, contacts.
- **Ask before touching** another department's gear.
- **Solve problems quietly.** A set works when everyone does their part without making noise.
- **Keep the contacts** of the people you work with.

## Tips for getting started

- **Try to get an in-person interview.** If that isn't possible, make it a Zoom or phone call. Your passion, enthusiasm and personality are your allies in making people want to work with you.
- **Be open to other areas.** If there's no room in the department you want, offer to work in another. Your area may be in high demand, but showing you're good and efficient in any department gets people to recognize what you're worth, and lets you meet the people in charge while you work. Your supervisors in that department will also be your allies when you need a recommendation.
- **Don't get discouraged if they don't call.** If you didn't get the project you applied for, follow up with the producers or heads of department every so often. If they don't hear from you, they'll forget you exist.
- **Personalize every message.** No generic text for everyone: who you're writing to, for which department and why you.
- **Show what you have.** Even if it's two short films, put them on your profile with your role and the year, and keep it up to date.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a university degree to work in film in the DR?

No. Many technical roles are learned on set and through specialized courses. Training in film, communications or a technical field helps, but people who hire look mostly at your credits, your command of the gear and your references.

### Do I need to speak English?

Not always for Dominican productions. For foreign productions, which are a big part of the work in the country, working English opens many more doors, especially in production, locations, camera and directing.

That said, there are very important local technicians who don't speak English. They have shown so much expertise in their field that they become invaluable, and their lack of the language becomes a tolerable limitation.

### Where do you find film jobs?

In the DR, most film jobs aren't published. They're filled through recommendations and direct contact with producers and heads of department. That's why reaching them matters so much.
